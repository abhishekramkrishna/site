Here's a puzzle many technology executives will recognise. Many employees say AI saves them real time on the tasks they use it for. Yet when leaders look for those gains in delivery, cost or output, they're hard to find.

Ethan Mollick, the Wharton professor who has studied AI at work more closely than most, offered an explanation at Workday Rising in October 2026: people are hiding it.

**The short version**

- People often hide their AI use because of outdated policies, pride, or fear that productivity gains lead to job cuts. The gains stay with individuals.
- Engineering teams are especially exposed, because engineers adopt tools early and on their own.
- Close the gap by using AI visibly as a leader, updating old AI policies, rewarding people who share techniques, and saying clearly where saved time will go.

## Why people hide their AI use

Mollick's argument is uncomfortable but persuasive. Some organisations still operate under policies written in 2023 that restrict or ban AI use, so using it means quietly breaking a rule. Some people don't want to admit that AI is doing the clever part of their job.

And some are simply making a rational calculation. As Mollick put it, "productivity gains are known to be a thing that precedes layoffs." If an employee discovers they can do a day's work in two hours, announcing it can feel risky. He noted that one of the most common uses of AI in surveys is to "work less and get paid the same amount."

The result is what he calls the overhang: the gap between what AI can already do and what organisations actually get from it.

## Why this matters for engineering leaders

Engineering teams are not immune. If anything, they are more exposed, because engineers adopt tools early and individually. The likely outcome is a team where a few people have quietly changed how they work, the techniques never spread, and leadership concludes AI isn't delivering.

The gains exist. They're just stuck with individuals.

## How to close the gap

Mollick suggests leaders need three things working together: leadership that uses AI openly and accepts some risk, broad experimentation across the team, and a deliberate effort to study how the work itself should change. In engineering terms, I'd translate that into four moves.

**Use it visibly yourself.** If leaders don't share how they use AI, nobody else will. Show your own prompts, your own failures and what you've stopped doing.

**Update the policy.** If your AI policy predates agentic tools, replace it. Make clear what is encouraged, not only what is prohibited.

**Reward sharing, not just output.** Make it normal for engineers to demo the workflow that saved them a day. Recognise the people who spread techniques, not only those who use them.

**Be honest about what happens to saved time.** This is the hardest one. If people believe efficiency leads to cuts, they will hide it. Say clearly where reclaimed time will go: quality, technical debt, the roadmap items that never get prioritised.

Mollick ended with a question I think every leader should ask their teams: "What have you stopped doing?" If the honest answer is "nothing", the organisation hasn't really adapted to AI yet, whatever the usage dashboards say.
